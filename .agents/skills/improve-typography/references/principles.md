# Principles and judgment

How to judge typography, when rules bend, and the habits to unlearn. Read before a full review or redesign, or when a rule seems to conflict with the brand.

From Butterick’s Practical Typography. Each heading links to the source page.

### [Why does typography matter?](https://practicaltypography.com/why-does-typography-matter.html)

- Treat reader attention as a precious, finite resource; don’t write as if you have unlimited attention.
- Use typography to help readers spend less attention on the mechanics of reading and more on your message.
- Don’t treat typography as more important than writing quality; use it to make good writing better.
- Assume readers judge documents by their typography, especially when attention is limited (Butterick’s First and Second Laws); don’t neglect it.

### [What is good typography?](https://practicaltypography.com/what-is-good-typography.html)

- Judge typography by how well it reinforces the meaning of the text, not by abstract aesthetic merit.
- Don’t rely on rote solutions; choices that work for one text won’t necessarily work for another.
- Understand the goals of your text first; good typography depends on that, not on taste.
- Prefer effective over pretty: typography that is pleasant but doesn’t reinforce meaning is a failure; ugly but effective typography is a success.
- Give the most visual prominence to the most important element of the message.
- Reject the idea that the best typography is “invisible.”

### [Where do the rules come from?](https://practicaltypography.com/where-do-the-rules-come-from.html)

- Hold your documents to the same standards as professionally published books, newspapers, and magazines; “there is only typography,” not separate academic, legal, or business typographies.
- Don’t accept technological excuses for poor typography.
- When choosing between more or less consistency with professional typography, choose more.
- Treat professional typography as a benchmark, not an all-or-nothing goal; take shortcuts where the effort outweighs the results.

### [Maxims of page layout](https://practicaltypography.com/maxims-of-page-layout.html)

- Decide first how the body text will look: point size, line length, line spacing, and font.
- Divide the page into foreground and background; don’t let background elements upstage the foreground. Distinguish them with position, size, font, and sometimes color.
- Make adjustments with the smallest visible increments.
- When in doubt, try it both ways; make samples and react visually rather than resolving decisions with logic.
- Be consistent: things that are the same should look the same; things that look different should actually be different.
- Relate each new element to existing elements; a grid can help.
- Keep it simple. If you think you need three colors and five fonts, a logo on every page, or clutter at the page edges, “think again.”
- Imitate typography you like.
- Don’t fear white space. Work outward from the text, not inward from the page edges.

### [Typewriter habits](https://practicaltypography.com/typewriter-habits.html)

- Use curly quotes and apostrophes, not straight ones.
- Use one space between sentences, not two; never type more than one word space at a time.
- Use real dashes, not multiple hyphens.
- Use real ™ and © symbols, not alphabetic approximations.
- Use the ellipsis character, not three periods.
- Use accented characters where they belong.
- Use tables, not tabs, for tabular layouts.
- Use spacing settings, not empty paragraphs, for vertical space.
- Use real math symbols, not alphabetic substitutes.
- Use real rules and borders, not repeated alphabetic characters.
- Use ligatures.
- Don’t underline anything.
- Use proportional fonts, not monospaced.
- Don’t abuse all caps.
- Use kerning and letterspacing.
- Avoid too much centered text.
- Use line spacing other than just single or double.

### [Are two spaces better than one?](https://practicaltypography.com/are-two-spaces-better-than-one.html)

- Use one space between sentences: “professional typographers always use one space between sentences.”
- Be aware of typographic conventions; relying on them helps persuade, and departing from them may have unintended consequences.
- Develop your own typographic judgment rather than copying his “cargo-cult style.”
- Cure the overuse of all caps, “a truly noxious habit.”
- Before designing, think critically about the intended reading context; e.g., turn off the lights while working on presentation slides.
- Use pragmatic considerations to narrow choices, then apply art and judgment to choose.
- When judging legibility research, ask what it tested as well as what it found.
- If you insist on extra space between sentences, use a wider Unicode whitespace character (e.g., U+2002) instead of two word spaces.

### [How to work with a designer](https://practicaltypography.com/how-to-work-with-a-designer.html)

- Don’t assume price correlates with quality.
- Spend plenty of time choosing a designer, be candid about expectations, then step aside and don’t micromanage.
- Find designers by reviewing online portfolios, asking colleagues, or tracking down the creators of work you like.
- If anything in a portfolio gives you pause, move on; a designer without a portfolio on their own domain signals “laziness or ineptitude.”
- It’s fine to ask for similar-client samples and a detailed proposal with deliverables and budget; negotiate a fair cancellation procedure (e.g., a pro rata kill fee).
- Don’t ask for discounts or spec work, or offer “exposure.”
- Don’t limit yourself to local designers; remote work is fine.
- Test all design work in context before approving; insist on real HTML pages for a website, not a Photoshop prototype.
- “Consensus kills”: give approval authority to the smallest possible group, never a popular vote.
